Accenture & Google Cloud

8 Sep 2026

Accenture and Google Cloud put 1,000 AI engineers inside client companies

What happened

Accenture and Google Cloud launched the Accenture Gemini Enterprise Business Group, a joint unit built around about 1,000 forward-deployed engineers: specialists who work inside client organisations to design, build and run AI systems on Google’s Gemini Enterprise platform. Google will train the engineers, and the unit will focus on enterprise adoption, repeatable industry solutions and moving clients from AI experiments to company-wide use. Accenture CEO Julie Sweet and Google Cloud CEO Thomas Kurian both framed the unit as a way to deliver AI outcomes at scale.

Why it matters

Enterprise AI is now being won at the deployment stage, not only at the model stage. TechCrunch cites Ramp data showing Google with about 6% of enterprise AI spending among Ramp’s US customers, against 43.5% for Anthropic and 39.7% for OpenAI, which explains why Google is buying reach through the largest integrator. For buyers, the choice of AI platform now often comes bundled with an embedded delivery team, and implementation capacity is becoming the scarce resource in the market.

TotalEnergies & Mistral AI

15 Sep 2026

TotalEnergies commits more than $115 million to a joint AI lab with Mistral AI

What happened

TotalEnergies and Mistral AI, the French AI model developer, launched a three-year programme worth more than $115 million (over EUR 100 million) to build AI models for oil and gas exploration, reservoir characterisation, field development and the optimisation of existing assets. The work runs as a joint scientific laboratory that pairs TotalEnergies’ subsurface specialists with Mistral’s researchers and engineers, and extends an existing collaboration. CEO Patrick Pouyanné said exploration and reservoir engineering are among the areas where AI can create the greatest value for the group.

Why it matters

The energy sector’s AI spending is moving from generic productivity tools into the core of how the business makes money: where to drill and how long fields keep producing. The deal also shows a preference among large European corporates for a European model partner and for proprietary models trained on their own data, which competitors cannot buy off the shelf.

Sysco

9 Sep 2026

Sysco launches a $500 million AI cost programme and ties long-term pay to it

What happened

Sysco, the global foodservice distribution leader, introduced a multi-year AI-powered efficiency programme targeting at least $500 million of savings by fiscal 2029, including $100 million of expected net savings already built into fiscal 2027 guidance. The programme covers truck routing and warehouse productivity, automation in merchandising and procurement, indirect spend, customer experience and back-office work. Achievement of the cost-out targets has been added to the company’s long-term equity performance plans. CEO Kevin Hourican presented the plan at the Barclays Global Consumer Staples Conference.

Why it matters

Tying AI savings to equity pay turns AI from an IT budget line into a management commitment with consequences. In a low-margin, high-volume business, the largest cost pools are physical (trucks, warehouses, buying), and that is where Sysco is pointing AI, not only at office work. These are targets, not results yet, but they now sit inside financial guidance.

Chewy

11 Sep 2026

Chewy puts a $50 million annual price tag on its AI savings

What happened

Chewy, the US online pet retailer, expects AI initiatives to deliver $50 million in annual cost savings from fiscal 2027, up from savings in the low tens of millions last year, executives said on its second-quarter earnings call. Its AI assistant Cai, released to a group of customers during the quarter, resolved about 30% of self-service chats about orders, accounts and the Autoship subscription. AI tools are also in use in customer care, pharmacy and vet services, including a voice assistant at Chewy Vet Care clinics.

Why it matters

Retailers are starting to report AI the way they report cost programmes: a dollar figure, a start date and a named driver. Once a few companies publish numbers like these, investors begin to expect AI savings in guidance, and projects that cannot be measured lose ground in budget rounds. The 30% chat resolution rate is also a useful early marker of what customer-service automation achieves in its first quarter.

Bank of America

24 Sep 2026

Bank of America says 140 AI projects returned twice their cost, and plans to double its AI budget

What happened

Bank of America plans to double its AI budget next year, executives said at September investor conferences. The bank said 140 AI implementations built for $400 million have generated $800 million in benefits, within about $4 billion a year spent on new technology initiatives. Its roughly 20,000 software developers use coding agents with gains in line with the 15-20% seen across most industries, and the Erica assistant handles internal self-service work equivalent to about 11,000 employees. Headcount fell from 213,000 to 209,000 this year; CEO Brian Moynihan said the bank is not laying anybody off and is managing hiring.

Why it matters

It is one of the few disclosures by a major bank that ties AI spending to a measured return, roughly two to one, across a whole portfolio rather than a single pilot. That sets a public benchmark peers will be measured against. It also shows how the payoff arrives in large banks: through slower hiring and attrition rather than layoffs.

Corporate venture capitalStrategic investments and corporate accelerators

Salesforce

2 Sep 2026

Salesforce makes its first investment in AI agent platform Wonderful, now valued at $5 billion

What happened

Wonderful, an Israeli-Dutch start-up whose platform coordinates AI agents, workflows and applications across a company’s data and systems, raised a $550 million Series C at a $5 billion valuation, more than double its $2 billion valuation of March 2026. Insight Partners led the round; Salesforce invested for the first time, alongside Index Ventures, IVP, Bessemer Venture Partners and others. The company started in customer-service AI and now operates in more than 35 countries.

Why it matters

Enterprise software incumbents are investing in the start-ups that could replace parts of their own products. Agent platforms that sit above existing applications challenge the per-seat software model, and a stake gives an incumbent a front-row view of how fast that shift is happening.

Samsung

8 Sep 2026

Samsung leads two of Europe’s largest AI funding rounds in one week

What happened

Samsung Electronics led Mistral AI’s EUR 3 billion Series D, which values the French model developer at EUR 21 billion, up from EUR 11.7 billion in 2025; Mistral called it the largest equity raise by a European technology company and says more than 125 enterprises in 20 countries use its technology. On 15 September Samsung also co-led a Series A of more than EUR 200 million in EUCLYD, a Dutch start-up designing energy-efficient chips and data centre systems for running large AI models, chaired by former ASML CEO Peter Wennink. The EQT-managed Scaleup Europe Fund invested in both rounds.

Why it matters

Corporate venture capital is now shaping which AI companies Europe ends up with. Samsung is positioning on both layers of the stack, the models and the hardware that runs them, and the same European growth fund sits beside it in both deals. For European AI start-ups, a strategic corporate investor is becoming the typical lead for the largest rounds.

Cohere & Aleph Alpha

16 Sep 2026

Cohere and Aleph Alpha sign their merger, anchored by Lidl owner Schwarz Group

What happened

Cohere, the Canadian enterprise AI model developer, and Aleph Alpha, the Heidelberg-based sovereign AI provider, signed a definitive business combination agreement, formalising a plan first disclosed in April. The combined company, reportedly valued at about $20 billion, will operate as Cohere with headquarters in Berlin and Toronto, and Heidelberg becomes a research centre. Schwarz Group, owner of Lidl and Kaufland, committed $600 million (EUR 500 million) in April as lead investor in Cohere’s next funding round, and the company will deliver sovereign AI on STACKIT, Schwarz’s cloud service. Closing needs regulatory approval.

Why it matters

A European retailer has become the strategic anchor of an AI lab, supplying capital, a cloud and an early customer base. It shows sovereignty moving from policy language into deal structure: control over where data and models run now drives consolidation among model developers, not only procurement decisions.

Vienna Insurance Group

23 Sep 2026

Vienna Insurance Group buys a 25% stake in its AI telematics partner

What happened

Vienna Insurance Group (VIG), the leading insurance group in Central and Eastern Europe, signed agreements to acquire 25% of Dolphin Technologies, a Vienna company founded in 2001 that builds AI-powered telematics platforms for insurers and carmakers: driving-style analysis, accident detection and predictive risk models. Seven VIG insurers already use its technology, including Compensa in Poland and Lithuania, Kooperativa in Czechia and Slovakia and Union in Hungary. The deal extends a partnership that began in 2022 and needs approval from the Austrian competition authority.

Why it matters

Insurers are moving from renting AI capabilities to owning a share of the supplier. A minority stake secures a technology already running in seven markets and gives the insurer a voice in its roadmap. In motor insurance, where pricing depends on driving data, owning part of the data platform is becoming a competitive position in its own right.

Gartner

1 Sep 2026

Only 22% of organisations have scaled AI beyond single business units, Gartner finds

What happened

A Gartner survey of 1,303 respondents at organisations with at least $50 million in revenue, run from January to April 2026, found that only 22% have scaled AI across multiple business units or adopted an AI-first approach. Functional leaders put an average of 12% of their budgets into AI last year, and 85% plan to spend more in 2026. High performers, which track returns continuously and stop weak projects, reported positive returns on 81% of their AI initiatives; low performers did not know the return on 29% of theirs.

Why it matters

Spending is running well ahead of scaling. The gap between leaders and the rest is about portfolio discipline, not technology: the same pattern behind Bank of America’s disclosed two-to-one return in this edition. As budgets grow, the ability to show a return per initiative is becoming the dividing line between AI programmes that expand and those that get cut.
